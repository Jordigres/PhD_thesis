\begin{abstract}

    Docked bike-sharing systems (BSSs) are an accessible, low-carbon mobility option that can replace short urban car trips and improve connectivity to public transport. As BSSs have evolved into large-scale services, performance increasingly depends on decisions across the system life cycle, from station placement to day-to-day operations. This thesis develops data-driven methods to support these tasks, using Barcelona and its docked BSS, \textit{Bicing}, as case studies.

    First, the thesis addresses station network design. Existing optimization approaches often fail to quantify system accessibility realistically, transparently explore trade-offs among diverse objectives (e.g., demand coverage and equity), or control station layout, since clustering versus dispersion typically emerges as a by-product of site selection. This thesis presents a flexible planning framework combining modular multi-criteria scoring with directed street-network accessibility metrics adjusted for topography, and uses a genetic algorithm to optimize station layouts while explicitly controlling inter-station proximity and overall accessibility. Applied to Barcelona, it identifies near-optimal configurations for alternative goals (coverage, connectivity, and equity) and shows that altitude-adjusted accessibility helps correct service gaps in hilly districts; the same approach is also used to identify new stations that complement an existing network.
    
    Turning to day-to-day operations, the thesis characterizes \textit{Bicing}’s mobility patterns, which underpin two predictive use cases. The first predictive use case targets maximizing electric bike availability by keeping batteries charged without exact route information or in-transit battery measurements. The thesis proposes a joint forecasting framework coupling a Markov-chain mobility model with a physics-based battery model to simulate trajectories and energy states, yielding accurate battery estimates and reliable short-horizon station-level forecasts that enable proactive charging strategies.
    
    The second use case targets anticipating bike component failures to reduce downtime, despite heterogeneous wear processes and limited data beyond routine operational logs. The thesis develops predictive maintenance models based on survival analysis and machine learning, using maintenance records, historical mobility indicators, and weather variables to estimate failure risk for key components (brake pads, wheel spokes, and chains). The models achieve consistently strong predictive performance and interpretability analysis shows that usage intensity is the main driver of degradation, with higher average speeds and electric propulsion further shortening component lifetimes.
    
    Overall, the thesis shows that docked BSS planning and operations can be improved with computational, data-driven decision support even under common data limitations. The proposed methods enable a shift from heuristic planning and reactive management to anticipatory, evidence-based strategies transferable to other cities with comparable spatial and operational data.
    

\textbf{Keywords:} bike-sharing, electric mobility, spatial optimization, data-driven methods, station placement, battery prediction, predictive maintenance, Barcelona, Bicing.

\end{abstract}
