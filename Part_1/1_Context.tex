\section{Context}
\label{sec:context}

Since the Industrial Revolution, technological innovation has transformed how societies produce, move, and live. Mechanization and mass manufacturing have increased productivity and population growth, while the introduction of railways, motor vehicles, and later aviation has enabled the faster movement of goods and people. Yet, alongside this progress--whose pace shows little sign of slowing--have come intensified resource consumption, widespread pollution, and unsustainable land use, all of which increasingly threaten the long-term viability of our societies by placing great pressure on the environment and human health~\cite{IPCC2023, UNEP2023}. 

These patterns have become particularly visible in cities, where rapid population growth has intensified the demand for housing, services, and infrastructure. Even though cities occupy just 3\% of the Earth's land, they already account for 60–80\% of energy consumption and 75\% of carbon emissions~\cite{UN_SDG11_2015}, and this tendency is expected to increase as by 2050, around 70\% of the world’s population is projected to live in urban areas, compared with about 57\% today~\cite{UN_SDG11_2015}. Such concentration of people and activity puts significant strain on resources and intensifies sustainability challenges. The \ac{sdg} recognize the urgency of this situation: Goal 11 aims to make cities inclusive, safe, resilient, and sustainable, emphasizing the need to transform urban spaces. Together, these facts highlight the need to rethink how cities are planned and managed.

Urban mobility is central in this challenge. In many metropolitan areas, car use has long been prioritized over more sustainable alternatives, with decades of urban planning favoring road expansion, parking, and low-density suburban growth instead of compact, mixed-use development. These choices have led to transport systems that emit high levels of \acp{ghg}, worsen air quality, create chronic congestion, and degrade the safety and livability of public spaces. In dense urban environments, where exposure to vehicle emissions and noise is greatest, the consequences for public health and social equity are especially severe. In response, many cities have adopted ambitious policies in the past decade, such as expanding \ac{pt}, cycling infrastructure, green space, and shared mobility services~\cite{UNHabitat2020}. Yet despite this progress, the transition to sustainable urban mobility remains incomplete.

Meanwhile, \ac{ai} and advanced data analytics have emerged as transformative forces across diverse domains leveraging techniques such as statistical modeling, \ac{ml}, \ac{dl}, and agent-based approaches to uncover hidden patterns, generate predictions, and optimize complex systems. Fueled by the growing availability of data from sensors, mobile devices, and digital platforms, \ac{ai}’s capabilities are also being harnessed in urban transport to tackle longstanding challenges such as traffic signal optimization~\cite{Meess2024}, multimodal trip planning~\cite{Liu2021}, and demand forecasting for various transport services~\cite{Ma2024, Gallo2019}.

Among the many strategies to promote sustainable urban solutions, \acp{bss} have emerged as one of the most visible examples of this shift. By providing flexible, low-carbon transport options, they have contributed to reducing car dependency and promoting active mobility. Moreover, the recent rollout of \acp{e-b} alongside traditional \acp{m-b} has further boosted their popularity. Recognizing their potential, researchers have turned to \ac{ai} to optimize key aspects of \ac{bss} operations, such as demand forecasting, fleet rebalancing, and station planning~\cite{Albuquerque2021, Bahadori2021}. However, substantial gaps remain in making \acp{bss} more efficient, equitable, and resilient.

This thesis explores how data-driven approaches can enhance the planning and operation of \acp{bss} as part of broader sustainable mobility strategies. Focusing on the case of Barcelona and its \ac{bss} Bicing, it develops and evaluates predictive models and optimization methods to address key challenges such as demand forecasting, battery level estimation, and the strategic placement of stations. Through these use cases, the research aims to demonstrate how \ac{ai} can support more efficient, equitable, and resilient \ac{bss} services, ultimately contributing to healthier and more sustainable urban environments.
