\section{Thesis outline and contributions}
\label{sec:thesis_outline_and_contributions}

\subsection{Thesis outline}

This thesis is organized into five parts:

\textbf{Part 1: Introduction.}
This part establishes the context and motivation for applying \ac{ai} techniques to docked \ac{bss}. It defines the research objectives and provides an overview of the thesis structure and core contributions.

\textbf{Part 2: Background.}
This part provides the conceptual and scientific foundations of the thesis, structured around four main chapters. First, it introduces the broader context of vehicle-sharing and focuses on \acp{bss}, covering their typologies, historical evolution, impacts, usage patterns, the integration of \ac{ai} into these systems, and the current main challenges and opportunities for \acp{bss}. Second, it presents a literature review on existing station placement strategies. Third, it examines the state of the art in mobility modeling and battery prediction for \ac{e-b} fleets. Finally, it reviews existing approaches to \ac{pm}, focusing on failure modeling techniques such as survival analysis and machine learning.

\textbf{Part 3: Data-driven station planning in Barcelona.}
This part begins by introducing the city of Barcelona as the case study for this research, outlining its existing \ac{bss} network, urban characteristics, and data availability. Building on this context, it then presents a flexible framework for optimizing the spatial placement of docking stations. The method combines a modular multi-criteria scoring system with network-based and topography-adjusted accessibility metrics, and applies a genetic algorithm to generate optimized station configurations that align with diverse planning objectives while respecting spatial and operational constraints.

\textbf{Part 4: Predictive operations in \textit{Bicing}.}  
Building on the operational data from Barcelona’s \ac{bss}, \textit{Bicing}, this part explores how predictive models can support more efficient system management. Two complementary applications are presented. First, a joint prediction framework is proposed to estimate \acp{e-b} locations and battery levels by combining a Markov-based mobility model with a physics-informed energy consumption model. Second, \ac{pm} models are developed to estimate failure risks in both \acp{m-b} and \acp{e-b}, using survival analysis techniques and interpretable features derived from historical usage and repair data.

\textbf{Part 5: Conclusion.}
The final part summarizes the contributions of the thesis and reflects on their implications for both researchers and \ac{bss} practitioners.


\subsection{Contributions}

The main contributions of this thesis, all rooted in the context of docked \acp{bss}, are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}  
    \item A flexible and modular data-driven framework for station planning that enables fine-grained spatial analysis and supports multi-criteria evaluation of candidate sites. The method integrates network-based accessibility metrics computed on directed, topography-aware street networks to reflect real-world cycling conditions. It also supports the incremental expansion and reconfiguration of existing station networks, allowing planners to explore trade-offs between competing objectives in a realistic and adaptable way.

    \item An approach for jointly predicting \acp{e-b} locations and battery levels in \acp{bss} fleets, combining a Markov-based mobility model with a physics-informed energy consumption model.

    \item A predictive maintenance strategy for \acp{bss}, based on interpretable survival models that estimate component-level failure risks for both \acp{m-b} and \acp{e-b}.

    \item Demonstration of all proposed methods through real-world applications in Barcelona and its \ac{bss} system, \textit{Bicing}, showing how \ac{ai} techniques can be operationalized to support decision-making in \acp{bss}.

\end{enumerate}
