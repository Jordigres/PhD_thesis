\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:conclusions_limitations}

Three main limitations qualify the scope of the findings presented in this thesis. Limitations specific to each contribution are already discussed in the corresponding chapters.

First, the contributions of this thesis were developed in a data-rich setting. Barcelona and \textit{Bicing} offer extensive open urban data and detailed operational records, whereas in many other cities data could be scarcer, fragmented, or more difficult to acquire. The contributions differ in how demanding they are in this respect. The station planning framework relies on widely available open data, and coarser inputs would reduce its spatial precision without preventing its application. The Markov mobility model requires only station-level \ac{od} trip records, which every docked \ac{bss} generates, although short histories would yield noisy transition probabilities. The battery model needs periodic battery-level records, which are less common, but has few parameters and was calibrated on only three weeks of data. The \ac{pm} framework is the most demanding, as it requires maintenance records linked to individual bikes and their usage; its sensitivity to data volume is already visible in the weaker predictions for chains, which had fewer recorded failures. Therefore, in data-poorer settings, the planning and mobility components would remain largely applicable, whereas the battery and maintenance components would first require operators to collect the necessary data.

Second, even where the required data are available, generalizability remains unverified. In principle, the approaches developed here are transferable, but the analyses and models have not been validated in other cities or \acp{bss}, so empirical transferability to different urban contexts has not been assessed. Furthermore, the work is framed in dense urban settings where \acp{bss} are typically deployed. Applicability to rural or low-density environments--where demand patterns, station spacing, and operational constraints differ substantially--has not been explored and remains an open question.

Third, all methods were evaluated on historical data only. The station planning framework has not been validated with stakeholder feedback, and the battery and maintenance predictions have not been tested in production. Assessing their real-world impact would require deployment and evaluation in live operator environments.
