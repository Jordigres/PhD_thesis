\section{Motivation and objectives}
\label{sec:motivation_and_objectives}

\subsection{Motivation}

The progressive adoption of \acp{bss} has transformed urban mobility by offering an affordable, sustainable alternative to motorized transport. As these systems expand, the growing availability of high-resolution mobility and operational data creates new opportunities to improve both day-to-day operations and long-term planning through computational and data-driven methods.

One of the most fundamental planning decisions concerns the strategic placement of docking stations, as their location strongly influences factors such as accessibility, ridership, and operational efficiency. Existing methods have made important advances by applying \ac{mcdm} techniques, demand forecasting, and spatial models to identify promising locations. However, many of these approaches rely on simplified assumptions or are designed in ways that limit their flexibility: planning goals are typically restricted to one or a couple of criteria; candidate sites are evaluated using aggregated spatial units rather than street-level data; and topography effects are often ignored. With the increasing availability of high-resolution spatial data, there is now a unique opportunity to overcome these limitations. The development of a flexible modelling framework would provide a valuable planning tool to support planners in exploring multiple scenarios and explicitly navigating trade-offs between competing objectives.

Beyond initial design, ongoing operations also present challenges, particularly with the progressive electrification of \ac{bss} fleets. While the introduction of \acp{e-b} increase system reach and appeal, they also require dedicated monitoring and redistribution strategies to ensure sufficient battery charge across the network. This is particularly challenging in dense or topographically diverse cities, where usage patterns and charging opportunities are unevenly distributed. Predictive models of battery levels can play a key role in supporting timely rebalancing and reducing the number of unusable vehicles in circulation.

At the same time, the maintenance data generated by the bikes offer a valuable foundation for improving fleet management. Rather than reacting to breakdowns after they occur, operators can now combine maintenance records with system usage patterns and environmental context to anticipate repair needs. Predictive models can help forecast when key components—such as brake pads, wheel spokes, or chains—are likely to fail, thereby improving workshop scheduling, reducing unnecessary downtime, and extending the useful life of the fleet.


\subsection{Objectives}

The main objective of this thesis is to develop computational data-driven methodologies to improve the design and operational planning of docked \acp{bss}. To achieve this overarching goal, the thesis is structured around the following specific objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{To investigate how \ac{mcdm} and spatial optimization can inform the placement of \ac{bss} stations.}  
    This includes examining how high-resolution spatial data, topography-adjusted distances, and network connectivity can be integrated into flexible approaches that reflect diverse and conflicting planning goals.

    \item \textbf{To analyze the spatiotemporal dynamics of \ac{e-b} battery levels and identify the main factors influencing depletion and charging patterns.}
    The aim is to better understand where and when battery-related imbalances emerge in the system and to explore modelling techniques that can support predictive fleet management.

    \item \textbf{To assess the feasibility of \ac{pm} models based on mobility and maintenance data.}  
    This involves examining how the \ac{bss} usage patterns and the contextual information can be used to estimate component wear and anticipate maintenance needs across \acp{e-b} and \acp{m-b}.
\end{enumerate}
