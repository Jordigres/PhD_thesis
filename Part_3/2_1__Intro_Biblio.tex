\section{Introduction}

\acp{bss} are becoming a key component of urban mobility, offering a sustainable and active mode of transport~\cite{Fishman2016, MeddinWorldMap2025}. As new systems are created and existing ones expand, a central planning challenge emerges: how to locate a limited number of stations so that the overall system performs effectively under specific planning priorities. This paper focuses on this strategic problem, framing it as follows. Given a set of feasible candidate locations, a fixed number of stations must be selected so as to maximize total system utility according to a planning goal. Here, we do not intend to locate stations solely based on estimates of individual trips or origin-destination flows. Rather, we propose a framework that supports strategic station placement when different priorities---such as demand-related factors, multimodal integration, or social equity---need to be explored within a single, consistent formulation.

Existing research has proposed numerous data-driven approaches to \ac{bss} station placement, including classical location-allocation models~\cite{Frade2015, Mete2018}, \ac{mcdm} methods~\cite{Kabak2018, Bahadori2022}, and both exact~\cite{Cintrano2018} and metaheuristic optimization techniques~\cite{Qian2022, Caggiani2020}. Regardless of the methodology used, the design and implementation of most docked systems still follow an iterative workflow in which the first and most critical step is the clear definition of planning objectives~\cite{Duran-Rodas2021, Ferrando2007, Yanocha2018}.

Previous research has substantially increased the analytical rigor of \ac{bss} planning relative to earlier, largely heuristic practice. Nevertheless, two persistent limitations remain. First, most models are formulated around a fixed objective, such as maximizing geographic coverage~\cite{Fazio2021} or demand coverage~\cite{Kabak2018, Bahadori2022}, and when multiple objectives are incorporated~\cite{Conrow2018, Qian2022, Caggiani2020, Duran-Rodas2021, Fan2024, Nikiforiadis2021}, the set of objectives is typically small and predefined, and adding a new objective usually requires reformulating the model. Second, station placement depends not only on the local suitability of each candidate site, but also on the system-wide structure of the chosen network, which includes how clustered stations are and how accessible the system. Despite the importance of these factors, in much of the literature they are not treated as an explicit component of the objective function, and therefore the optimization cannot prioritize one or the other properly. Beyond these two limitations, many studies rely on coarse spatial units—such as administrative zones or regular grids~\cite{Frade2015, Fazio2021, Veillette2018, Nikiforiadis2021}—and often evaluate distances using euclidean metrics or undirected networks~\cite{Cintrano2018, Xin2013}, which may not fully reflect the reality of bicycle networks in cities with one-way streets. Moreover, topography is often not accounted for~\cite{Fazio2021, Kabak2018, Ebrahimi2022, Tera2023}.

To address these limitations, this paper develops \textit{FlexBSS}, a station placement framework built around four complementary components: First, it introduces a general \ac{mcdm}-based scoring scheme that quantifies the suitability of each candidate location by aggregating spatial attributes, allowing planners to incorporate any relevant factor and to assign weights that reflect diverse planning priorities within a single formulation. Second, it integrates network-level metrics computed on a directed bicycle graph, enabling explicit evaluation and tuning of both station dispersion and system-wide accessibility while respecting one-way streets and routing constraints. Third, it incorporates topography-adjusted distance calculations that account for slope-induced variations in cycling effort, improving the realism of proximity and accessibility assessments. Finally, the model is designed to support not only new system deployments but also system expansions and reconfigurations by explicitly accounting for the current station layout and its network relationships. The resulting combinatorial optimization problem---formulated with binary decision variables---is solved using a \ac{ga}, which enables the exploration of diverse planning scenarios for large candidate sets within reasonable computational time.

To demonstrate the applicability of this framework, we conduct a case study for the city of Barcelona, Spain, and its public \ac{bss}, \textit{Bicing}, using its extensive open data resources. We illustrate both the deployment of a new station network under three different planning scenarios and the expansion of an existing system using the l'Eixample district. The results demonstrate how the approach helps planners systematically explore alternative configurations, assess trade-offs among multiple planning objectives, and design station networks tailored to the city's socioeconomic context, infrastructure and topography.

\section{Literature review}

The siting of \ac{bss} stations has been addressed through a wide range of methodological perspectives. Contributions in the literature vary in terms of the data they use, the analytical tools they apply, and the planning objectives they pursue. This section reviews these contributions to clarify the main ways the problem has been addressed and to highlight insights relevant to this study.

\subsection{From heuristics to computational planning}

Docked \ac{bss} planning is typically an iterative process that begins with clearly defining the planning goals, such as maximizing ridership, promoting social equity, or improving first-last kilometer access~\cite{Duran-Rodas2021, Ferrando2007, Yanocha2018}. These goals should be used to guide each stage of site selection and refinement~\cite{Ferrando2007, Yanocha2018}, drawing on demand patterns, system performance measures, and stakeholder feedback. In practice, early \ac{bss} deployments relied on expert judgment and heuristics to guide site selection, with planning guides recommending stations in visible, accessible, and well-connected high-demand areas and encouraging stakeholder input~\cite{Ferrando2007, Yanocha2018, CROW2006}. Furthermore, cycling infrastructure manuals emphasized coherent, continuous cycling networks~\cite{AASHTO1999bicycle, CROW2006}. While these guidelines provide valuable practical recommendations, they lack analytical tools or optimization frameworks capable of systematically evaluating locations across diverse urban conditions. Nonetheless, heuristics and optimization tools can complement each other: practical rules can inform modelling choices, while optimization results can be used to refine or validate heuristic guidelines.

As the availability of detailed spatial data and advanced computational tools increased in the early 2010s, researchers moved beyond relying solely on expert judgment and heuristics. They began to apply spatial optimization models, particularly location-allocation formulations, which approach station siting as a mathematical problem. The following sections review these developments.


\subsection{Location-allocation models} \label{sec:location_allocation_models}

Location-allocation models are spatial optimization tools that place a fixed number of facilities and allocate demand to them to optimize one performance metric~\cite{Daskin1995}. Demand is typically defined over zones or grid cells and facilities over candidate sites, usually as discrete-location problems~\cite{Teixeira2008}. Core formulations include the \textit{p}-median problem, which minimizes total demand-weighted distance~\cite{Hakimi1965}; the \ac{mclp}, which maximizes demand served within a service radius (i.e., a threshold within which users are considered covered)~\cite{Church1974}; and the \textit{p}-center problem, which minimizes the maximum distance between users and their nearest facility~\cite{Hakimi1964}.

Several studies have applied these formulations to \ac{bss} placement~\cite{Frade2015, Cintrano2018, Lin2011, Mete2018}. In these cases, they typically rely on single-objective optimization frameworks, most often aiming to either maximize spatial coverage or minimize user distance, while incorporating practical constraints. For instance, Cintrano et al.~\cite{Cintrano2018} applied the \textit{p}-median problem; Frade and Ribeiro~\cite{Frade2015} used an \ac{mclp} variant to maximize population coverage under budget constraints, optimizing station capacity and locations, and fleet size; Lin and Yang~\cite{Lin2011} applied a \ac{minlp} model minimizing system costs that account for travel distance, installation, inventory, and unmet demand; and Mete et al.~\cite{Mete2018} used several coverage-based formulations (\textit{p}-center, \textit{p}-median, set covering).

While these models offer reproducible alternatives to heuristic siting, they remain focused on geographical coverage and user-to-station distance, and thus, broader priorities such as social equity or multimodal integration are largely absent. The following sections review how other approaches address such goals. 


\subsection{Combining multiple data sources} \label{sec:combining_multiple_data_sources}

Many studies combine multiple data sources in the station placement process in order to pursue diverse planning objectives and thus face the question of how to aggregate or balance them. The literature structures this in two main ways. In \textit{multi-criteria} approaches, multiple data sources are combined in support of one planning objective; in \textit{multi-objective} approaches, factors that pertain to different goals are kept separate and trade-offs between them are made explicit. This section reviews both.

In \textit{multi-criteria} studies, the aim is a single planning objective--usually maximizing demand coverage--and the information used to pursue it comes from several sources. Variables such as population density, proximity to \ac{pt}, and cycling infrastructure are typically weighted and aggregated to support that objective. Bahadori et al.~\cite{Bahadori2021} group usual factors into bike network, operator, user, and city infrastructure. Because these are in different units and play different roles, studies must choose how to weight and combine them. Some studies rely on structured judgment: \ac{ahp}, Fuzzy \ac{ahp}, \ac{moora}, \ac{bwm}, or \ac{topsis} are used to prioritize criteria with stakeholders~\cite{Kabak2018, Guler2021a, Guler2021b, Bahadori2022}. Others derive weights from data using statistical models of observed \ac{bss} usage (e.g.\ regression coefficients or variable importance scores in \ac{ml})~\cite{Wang2021}. Others use either equal~\cite{Fazio2021, Tera2023} or flexible~\cite{Veillette2018} weights for different factors, emphasizing that these should be tailored to local priorities and set in consultation with stakeholders.

This logic of including multiple factors to support a single objective appears in two main forms. In the first, suitability maps are built without formal optimization methods to rank and select candidate station locations. For instance, Fazio et al.~\cite{Fazio2021} do this for cycle parking in Catania, aggregating thematic indexes into a composite score across the study area. Similarly, Kabak et al.~\cite{Kabak2018} use \ac{ahp} and \ac{moora} and Tera et al.~\cite{Tera2023} use MULTIMOORA. In the second form, the same idea is embedded in an optimization model: multiple spatial factors are combined into one objective, which is then optimized within a location-allocation or related framework. García-Palomares et al.~\cite{GarciaPalomares2012} combine building-level and zonal data in a slope-aware network to estimate demand, applying $p$-median and \ac{mclp}; Chou et al.~\cite{Chou2019} combine \ac{pt} data in a network flow model with proportional flow constraints to align station locations and dock capacity with latent short-distance demand applying a mixed integer programming model; and Ebrahimi et al.~\cite{Ebrahimi2022} use \ac{mclp} and Target Market Share models to evaluate coverage and identify accessibility gaps.

\textit{Multi-objective} approaches, by contrast, treat different planning goals as distinct. No single solution optimizes all objectives at once; instead, the outcome is a set of Pareto-optimal solutions. This separation makes the contribution of each objective explicit and enables systematic exploration of alternatives, rather than concealing them within a single aggregated metric~\cite{Rahimi2023, honegumi}. 

Studies differ in which objectives they treat. For instance, Conrow et al.~\cite{Conrow2018} maximize coverage of both residential population and bicycle network infrastructure, but a recurring theme is social equity, with several studies balancing demand with equity. Duran-Rodas et al.~\cite{Duran-Rodas2021} generate alternative plans under different emphases on demand versus equity to illustrate how prioritizing demand, social equity, or a blend of both reshapes allocation; Caggiani et al.~\cite{Caggiani2020} minimize disparities in accessibility across population groups using a Theil index of multimodal accessibility; Fan and Harper~\cite{Fan2024} jointly maximize demand coverage and \ac{pt} for underserved communities, showing that a modest emphasis on equity can yield large gains in accessibility for disadvantaged populations with minimal system-wide loss; and Qian et al.~\cite{Qian2022} focus on reallocation of existing stations to improve both revenue and equitable access to jobs and essential services, particularly for disadvantaged communities, employing a \ac{ga} to explore configurations. Multi-objective approaches have also been used to address operational goals, such as Nikiforiadis et al.~\cite{Nikiforiadis2021}, who use a two-phase framework: in the first phase each objective (demand coverage, area coverage, and rebalancing needs) is optimized independently to establish benchmarks, and in the second phase planners minimize weighted percentage deviations from those targets. In these multi-objective approaches, trade-offs between objectives are kept explicit rather than folded into one composite, allowing systematic exploration.

Together, multi-criteria and multi-objective work has clarified how multiple data sources and goals can be brought into \ac{bss} placement. In both strands, however, the set of factors or objectives is usually fixed in advance; adding new dimensions would require reformulating the model. To address this, we introduce a modular \ac{mcdm} framework that allows new criteria to be incorporated without changing the overall structure, and flexible weights so that different planning objectives can be emphasized as needed.


\subsection{Spatial granularity and network representation}

Studies vary in the spatial scope of the planning area and in the granularity of candidate site definition. In terms of scope, analyses range from entire cities~\cite{Conrow2018, Duran-Rodas2021} to districts~\cite{GarciaPalomares2012} or campuses~\cite{Mete2018}. 

Common granularity choices include zonal formulations~\cite{Frade2015, Mete2018, Conrow2018}, grid-based studies~\cite{Fazio2021, Tera2023, Veillette2018} (e.g.\ 100\,m or 300\,m grids), hybrid combinations of grid cells with zones and transport nodes~\cite{Nikiforiadis2021}, and Voronoi-based catchment zones~\cite{Duran-Rodas2021}. These approaches improve tractability by simplifying the problem but sacrifice spatial precision, as the exact placement within each cell or zone remains unspecified. A second group of studies uses network-based approaches, selecting candidates along major corridors, at street intersections, or near points of interest such as \ac{pt} stops. Conrow et al.~\cite{Conrow2018}, Caggiani et al.~\cite{Caggiani2020}, and Fan and Harper~\cite{Fan2024} place candidate sites along the bicycle or \ac{pt} network, offering more spatial detail than coarse zoning but without enumerating every possible location. To our knowledge, only a few studies pursue high-resolution approaches by using street-network-derived candidate sets for station locations. Cintrano et al.~\cite{Cintrano2018} enumerate 33,550 street segments across Málaga as potential sites, while Xin~\cite{Xin2013} evaluates 1,489 street intersections within Vancouver's downtown area. 

Beyond site definition, some studies use network representations to evaluate travel paths rather than euclidean or zonal approximations~\cite{GarciaPalomares2012, Cintrano2018, Xin2013}, improving on simpler models. Networks are typically represented as undirected, with links treated as bidirectional and of equal length in both directions. This simplification omits one-way streets and asymmetric accessibility but still captures cycling conditions better than non-network models.

Additionally, slope strongly influences \ac{bss} cycling behaviour, with fewer trips observed on uphill trips and more on downhill ones~\cite{Morency2015, MateoBabiano2016}, especially in cities with heterogeneous topography, such as Barcelona~\cite{Grau-Escolano2024} and Brisbane~\cite{MateoBabiano2016}. In terms of modelling, García-Palomares et al.~\cite{GarciaPalomares2012} took an important step by incorporating slope as a uniform penalty on effective travel time, but their approach does not distinguish between uphill and downhill travel, and therefore does not capture the increased effort required for going uphill, nor the facilitating effect of moderate declines or the safety-related penalties of steep descents.

Our work builds directly on previous high-resolution approaches that derive candidate sites from the street network, such as Cintrano et al.~\cite{Cintrano2018} and Xin~\cite{Xin2013}, as well as on the early recognition by García-Palomares et al.~\cite{GarciaPalomares2012} of the need to incorporate slope to estimate more realistic user access distances. Specifically, we address two key limitations. First, unlike prior work that relied on undirected networks, we employ a directed street-level representation that captures circulation constraints such as one-way streets, as shortest routes in opposite directions can differ substantially in length, with empirical evidence showing asymmetries of more than 10\% in some urban areas~\cite{Melo2022}. Second, we replace the uniform slope penalty of García-Palomares et al. with an effort-based distance measure that differentiates between uphill and downhill gradients. Empirical studies have shown that uphill slopes strongly reduce speed in a near-linear fashion, whereas downhill effects are non-linear: cyclists accelerate only up to a certain gradient and often brake on steeper descents due to safety concerns~\cite{Ryeng2016, Flugel2019}. By incorporating these asymmetries, our approach improves the realism of the model.


\subsection{Spatial arrangement of stations}

An important \ac{bss} design aspect is the spatial arrangement of stations, that is, how stations are distributed throughout the service area. Classical network-based models, such as the $p$-median or $p$-center problems~\cite{Hakimi1964, Hakimi1965}, are typically formulated to enhance accessibility. In these approaches, the arrangement of facilities emerges as a side effect of optimizing for accessibility, rather than as a goal that planners can directly control. This pattern also holds in \ac{bss} applications of these models~\cite{Frade2015, Cintrano2018, Lin2011, Mete2018}, where, despite differences in implementation, efforts are primarily directed toward improving accessibility. As a result, the spatial distribution of stations is treated as a byproduct of the optimization process, not as an explicit planning objective in its own right.

Beyond accessibility, a complementary dimension of spatial arrangement is proximity, reflecting how close stations are to one another. Accessibility and proximity are distinct dimensions: a tightly clustered set of stations at one edge of the service area may have high inter-station proximity but low accessibility for most users, whereas relocating the same cluster to a central area leaves proximity unchanged while improving accessibility. Proximity can support short trips and rebalancing, yet most models impose only minimum inter-station distances rather than explicitly optimizing proximity~\cite{Nikiforiadis2021, Kabak2018}. Concepts from network science such as farness~\cite{Sabidussi1966}—the sum of shortest-path distances from a node to all others—quantify spatial separation among stations and offer a principled way to evaluate inter-station spacing, complementing accessibility.

Building on earlier work that focused on accessibility or minimum spacing~\cite{Frade2015, Cintrano2018, Lin2011, Mete2018, Nikiforiadis2021, Kabak2018}, our framework combines accessibility and proximity in a weighted composite score integrated with the \ac{mcdm} framework, making spatial arrangement trade-offs explicit and allowing broader planning goals to be reflected.


\subsection{Algorithmic approaches}

The suitability scoring and ranking methods~\cite{Kabak2018, Bahadori2022, Guler2021a, Guler2021b} discussed above support visualization and ranking of candidate sites. However, they do not yield a single optimal configuration. To obtain one, the problem must be formulated as an optimization problem, where station placement is framed as maximizing or minimizing a defined objective subject to constraints. As described earlier, common formulations include the $p$-median, maximal covering, and $p$-center models. These problems are typically set up as integer programmes with binary decision variables indicating whether a station is placed at each candidate location~\cite{Frade2015, Lin2011, Mete2018, Cintrano2018}, and constraints such as budget limits or a fixed number of stations~\cite{Conrow2018, Nikiforiadis2021, Caggiani2020}. These models are typically solved with exact solvers such as CPLEX~\cite{Mete2018, Chou2019}, Gurobi~\cite{Mix2022}, or similar~\cite{Lin2011, Conrow2018}, which guarantee optimality but can struggle as the candidate set or problem complexity grows. Some studies therefore use the location-allocation tools in \ac{gis} software such as ArcGIS~\cite{GarciaPalomares2012, Ebrahimi2022}, which implement heuristics for $p$-median or covering problems and can quickly generate near-optimal solutions.

To address the computational limitations of exact optimization methods, researchers in various fields have increasingly adopted metaheuristic algorithms~\cite{Tomar2023}. Techniques such as \ac{ga}, \ac{simann}, \ac{pso}, and \ac{aco} are now widely used for tackling large-scale combinatorial optimization problems, with applications in facility location~\cite{Chadry2003, Chalupa2019}, transport planning~\cite{Holliday2023, Sachan2024}, and network design~\cite{Gallo2010, Madadi2024}. While these methods were originally developed for single-objective problems, they have been adapted for multi-objective optimization by approaches like weighted aggregation or the $\varepsilon$-constraint method~\cite{Rahimi2023}. However, such adaptations often require multiple algorithm runs to approximate the Pareto front and may fail when trade-offs are non-convex or discontinuous. To overcome these limitations, dedicated multi-objective metaheuristics (e.g.\ \ac{nsgaii}~\cite{Deb2002}, \ac{spea2}~\cite{Zitzler2001} or \ac{mopso}~\cite{Coello2002}) have been developed to approximate the Pareto front in a single run by maintaining a set of nondominated solutions. In \ac{bss}, single-objective metaheuristics have been used to optimize station placement, with \acp{ga} applied in particular to $p$-median and multi-objective problems~\cite{Cintrano2018, Qian2022, Liu2015, Caggiani2020}. Comparative work suggests that \acp{ga} can outperform alternatives such as \ac{simann}, \ac{pso}, or Iterated Local Search on $p$-median formulations~\cite{Cintrano2018}. Moreover, hybrid \ac{ga}--\ac{pso} approaches have also been applied~\cite{Askari2017, Chen2020}.

Metaheuristic algorithms are typically validated by solution quality, with additional consideration for computational time and memory use~\cite{Osaba2021}. Because they are stochastic, it is standard to perform multiple independent runs and report summary statistics (e.g., best, mean, and standard deviation of the objective value) to assess reliability~\cite{Osaba2021}. Solution quality is then evaluated in two main ways: when the optimal solution or a tight bound is known, the relative difference between the metaheuristic's best-found value and the optimum is used~\cite{Roshanaei2010}; when exact solutions are unavailable or too costly to obtain, the metaheuristic is compared against simpler heuristics or other metaheuristic results. Moreover, comparative studies often use statistical tests to determine whether observed performance differences are significant~\cite{Derrac2011, Danka2013}.

In this framework, we adopt a \ac{ga} due to (i) its prior use in \ac{bss} optimization; (ii) its ability to incorporate custom, constraint-aware operators; (iii) its suitability for encoding solutions at the street-network level; and (iv) its stronger performance in preliminary tests. Furthermore, in contrast to most previous \ac{bss} optimization studies, which rarely compare metaheuristic solutions against alternative methods, we evaluate the proposed \ac{ga} by benchmarking it against a set of baseline approaches, providing evidence of its relative performance.
