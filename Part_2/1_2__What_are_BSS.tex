
\section{What are bike-sharing systems?}
\label{sec:what_are_bss}

\acp{bss} are mobility services that provide access to shared bicycles for short periods of time, enabling users to complete individual trips without owning a bicycle~\cite{Fishman2016}. The principle is simple: pick up a bike, ride it to the desired destination, and leave it for the next user. By removing the burdens of ownership—such as storage, maintenance, and theft risk—\acp{bss} make cycling more accessible to a wider range of users, including those without personal bicycles or secure storage. These systems are promoted as a way to normalize and expand the role of cycling in urban transport. Despite relatively high bicycle ownership rates in many regions, cycling still accounts for less than 5\% of daily trips in most countries~\cite{Chen2022}, revealing a significant gap between potential and actual use. By providing convenient, low-commitment access to bicycles, \acp{bss} aim to lower key barriers to cycling and position it as a practical alternative to private motorized travel. At the same time, they offer cities a scalable and visible tool to advance broader goals in sustainability, public health, and urban livability~\cite{Shaheen2010, Ricci2015}.

Contemporary \acp{bss} are generally classified as \textbf{docked} (or station-based) or \textbf{dockless} (or free-floating). Docked systems require users to pick up and return bicycles at designated docking stations, while dockless ones allow bikes to be parked freely within defined areas using built-in locks and GPS technology. In either case, modern systems typically comprise four main components: (i) a fleet of bicycles; (ii) pick-up and return infrastructure—docking stations in docked systems or designated parking areas in dockless systems; (iii) user access mechanisms; and (iv) operational systems for fleet maintenance, monitoring, and redistribution. Their flexible, on-demand nature supports a variety of trip purposes, including full commuting trips, first–last mile connections within multimodal journeys, short errands, and recreational rides.

In practice, contemporary \acp{bss} exhibit a wide range of configurations depending on local priorities, governance models, and technological choices~\cite{Shaheen2010, Ricci2015, Fishman2016}. As previously mentioned, systems may be docked, dockless, or even hybrid, combining fixed docking hubs with flexible drop-off zones. Fleets may consist of 
\acp{m-b}, \acp{e-b}, or comprise both bicycle types, while pricing models often blend pay-per-use and subscription options and may offer incentives for short trips. Regardless of these differences, nowadays most \acp{bss} rely on real-time monitoring, preventive maintenance, and fleet rebalancing to ensure reliable service.

Beyond their technical setup, bike-sharing systems also differ in how they are owned, managed and funded. Ownership and management models in bike-sharing can be broadly grouped into three modalities~\cite{Li2021, Cao2022}. Public systems are fully owned and managed by public organizations; Madrid’s \textit{Bicimad}, run directly by the city’s transport company EMT, is a clear example~\cite{BiciMAD2025}. At the other extreme, private systems are owned and operated by companies such as \textit{Lime} and \textit{Donkey Republic}. Between these two poles, there are cities that have adopted hybrid public–private partnerships, where ownership remain in public hands but operations are outsourced to private firms. Barcelona’s \textit{Bicing}, owned by the municipality but operated under concession by \textit{Pedalem Barcelona}~\cite{BicingPedalem2017}, illustrates this model. Funding structures reflect these arrangements: public systems are typically supported by municipal budgets or subsidies, private systems rely on user fees and advertising revenues, and hybrid models combine both sources~\cite{Shaheen2010}.
